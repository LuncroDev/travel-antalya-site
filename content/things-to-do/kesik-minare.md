The Kesik Minare, or "Broken Minaret", stands in the middle of Kaleiçi, Antalya's walled old town. It is one site with many lives: a public square in antiquity, a Byzantine church, a mosque, and then a fire-gutted ruin for well over a century. Since a long restoration it is a working mosque again, the **Şehzade Korkut Camii**, and it is open to visitors. We used the Ministry of Culture and Tourism's March 2025 press release, the metropolitan municipality's old-town guide, the TDV encyclopedia entry and news reports on the restoration, all read on 28 November 2026.

## Kesik Minare: what you are looking at

Antalya Metropolitan Municipality says the site was used in the Hellenistic period and served as the city's **agora** in Hellenistic and Roman times. An early Byzantine church dedicated to the Virgin, the **Panhagia**, was built inside it and became, in the municipality's words, the holiest place in Pamphylia in the middle Byzantine period. The ministry's 2025 release dates the church to the 6th–7th century AD.

The church became a mosque, and the name most sources attach to it is **Şehzade Korkut**, the Ottoman prince who governed the region in the reign of Bayezid II. The record is not tidy. A 2020 Anadolu Agency report quotes the regional foundations director as saying the Seljuks first converted it in 1207, and the TDV encyclopedia notes a spell as a Latin church under the Cypriot kingdom in the late Middle Ages. The ministry counts six restorations or additions over the building's history.

Then came the fire. Most sources, including the ministry, give **1896**; the municipality writes 1895 and the ministry's own English Antalya page says 1851. The fire ruined the building and broke the top off the minaret, which gave it its nickname. The TDV encyclopedia, written before the restoration, describes only the stone minaret standing, up to the level of its balcony.

## The restoration

For decades the site stood behind railings. The General Directorate of Foundations (Vakıflar) put the restoration out to tender in **April 2017**, working with the Antalya governorship, and news reports describe a scientific board of about 80 people. According to the ministry, more than **1,300 stones** were taken down, catalogued, numbered and put back one by one.

The aim was what reports call a "museum-mosque". Glass panels in parts of the building let you see the older remains underneath, and excavations found a **monopteros**, a small round temple ringed by a single row of columns. The courtyard now works as an open-air display of the architectural stones. A new cone was placed on the minaret in **2019**, the first time it had a top since the fire.

The first prayer in the restored mosque was held on **10 January**, according to a DHA report dated 11 January 2022 (Wikipedia gives an earlier date, 5 March 2021, which the 2021 news coverage does not support). In March 2025 Culture and Tourism Minister Mehmet Nuri Ersoy attended Friday prayers there and said the building was now open "both for worship and for visits".

## Visiting the Kesik Minare

- **Where:** on Hesapçı Sokak, the lane that runs south through Kaleiçi to [Hıdırlık Tower](/things-to-do/hidirlik-tower/).
- **Hours and tickets:** no official page publishes opening hours or a fee. It is a working mosque, so expect it to be open during the day and busy at prayer times.
- **Etiquette:** cover shoulders and knees, women should bring a scarf for the prayer hall, and take shoes off where asked. Avoid the noon prayer on Fridays if you want to look around.
- **Photos:** the minaret is easy to photograph from the lane; ask before photographing people inside.

The ministry has not said whether the courtyard remains and glass panels can be seen at all times, so check at the door.

## Best time to go

Kaleiçi is at its quietest in the morning, before the day-trip groups and the heat. Late afternoon light is good on the pale limestone of the minaret. In winter the old town is calm all day, which makes it a good rainy-day stop between museums.
